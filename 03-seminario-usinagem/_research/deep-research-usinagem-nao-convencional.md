## 1. NON-CONVENTIONAL MACHINING METHODS: DEFINITIONS AND CLASSIFICATION

### 1.1 Overview and Rationale

Non-conventional (or non-traditional) machining processes are manufacturing methods that remove material without relying on conventional cutting tools making physical contact with the workpiece. They emerged to address limitations of conventional machining when dealing with:

- Extremely hard materials (hardness > 45 HRC)
- Complex geometries impossible to achieve with mechanical tools
- Very thin or fragile workpieces
- Micro-scale features (sub-millimeter)
- Materials that are difficult to clamp or hold

The standard classification organizes these processes by their **primary energy source** (Benedict, 2017; Youssef & El-Hofy, 2020; Davim, 2013):

### 1.2 MECHANICAL ENERGY PROCESSES

#### 1.2.1 Ultrasonic Machining (USM)

**Principle:** A tool vibrates at high frequency (18-40 kHz) with low amplitude (0.05-0.125 mm). Abrasive particles suspended in a water slurry are driven against the workpiece surface, removing material through microchipping via repeated impact of abrasive grains.

**Typical Materials Processed:**
- Ceramics (alumina, zirconia, silicon carbide)
- Glass and quartz
- Precious stones
- Hardened steels (>45 HRC)
- Tungsten carbide

**Precision/Tolerances:**
- Dimensional accuracy: +/-5 to +/-25 micrometers
- Surface roughness (Ra): 1.5-2.5 micrometers
- Cutting rates vary by material: Soda-lime glass ~26.5 micrometers/s; Alumina ~3.8 micrometers/s; Zirconia ~2.3 micrometers/s

**Abrasives Used:** Boron carbide, aluminum oxide, silicon carbide (grain sizes 100-1000, suspended 20-60% by volume in water)

**Advantages:**
- No thermal damage to workpiece
- No chemical alteration of material properties
- Produces burr-free surfaces
- Handles brittle materials that would fracture under conventional tooling
- Complex non-uniform shapes achievable

**Limitations:**
- Very slow material removal rate
- Sonotrode (tool) wear from abrasive impact
- Ineffective for deep holes (slurry cannot penetrate)
- Limited to materials with hardness >45 HRC
- Not cost-effective for soft metals

**Key References:**
- Pei, D., Prabhakar, D., Ferreira, P.M. (1995). "Rotary ultrasonic drilling and milling of ceramics."
- Yang, Z., Zhu, L., et al. (2019). "Grinding force modeling in ultrasonic vibration assisted grinding of ZrO2." *Ceramics International*. (186 citations)
- Das, S., Doloi, B., Bhattacharyya, B. (2013). "Optimisation of ultrasonic machining of zirconia bio-ceramics using genetic algorithm." *Int. J. Machine Tools and Manufacture*.

#### 1.2.2 Abrasive Jet Machining (AJM)

**Principle:** A focused stream of fine abrasive particles (typically Al2O3 or SiC, 10-50 micrometers) is propelled by high-pressure gas (air or CO2 at 2-8 bar) through a small nozzle (0.3-0.5 mm) at velocities of 150-300 m/s against the workpiece surface, eroding material through repeated impact.

**Typical Materials Processed:**
- Ceramics and glass
- Semiconductors
- Thin metals and foils
- Composites

**Precision/Tolerances:**
- Accuracy: +/-50 to +/-125 micrometers
- Surface roughness: dependent on particle size and velocity
- Kerf width: 0.1-0.3 mm

**Advantages:**
- No thermal or mechanical stress on workpiece
- Low capital cost
- Good for cleaning, deburring, deflashing
- Suitable for thin and fragile materials
- No heat-affected zone

**Limitations:**
- Low material removal rate
- Nozzle wear
- Stray particle damage (requires masking)
- Limited to shallow cuts
- Dust control required

**Key References:**
- Wakuda, M., Yamauchi, Y., Kanzaki, S. (2002). "Effect of workpiece properties on machinability in abrasive jet machining of ceramic materials." *Precision Engineering*. (140 citations)
- Haldar, B., et al. (2018). "Abrasive jet system and its various applications." *Materials Today: Proceedings*. (35 citations)

#### 1.2.3 Abrasive Water Jet Machining (AWJM)

**Principle:** Ultra-high-pressure water (210-690 MPa / 30,000-100,000 psi) is forced through a jewel orifice creating a coherent stream at approximately Mach 3 (760 m/s). Abrasive particles (garnet or aluminum oxide) are entrained in the stream, combining hydraulic force with abrasive cutting action.

**Typical Materials Processed:**
- Virtually any material: metals, ceramics, stone, composites, glass
- Steel up to 150 mm thick
- Most materials up to 460 mm thick
- Exceptions: tempered glass, diamonds

**Precision/Tolerances:**
- Accuracy: down to 0.13 mm (0.005 in)
- Repeatability: 0.025 mm (0.001 in)
- Kerf width (abrasive): 1.0-1.3 mm, narrowing to 0.51 mm
- Kerf width (pure water): 0.18-0.33 mm, as fine as 0.076 mm
- Taper: less than 1 degree

**Advantages:**
- No heat-affected zone (cold cutting)
- Cuts virtually any material
- Minimal material distortion
- Environmentally friendly (garnet is non-toxic, recyclable)
- Intricate cuts with sharp corners possible
- Omni-directional cutting

**Limitations:**
- Cannot cut tempered glass or diamonds
- Edge quality varies with speed (trade-off: speed vs. finish)
- High operating costs (abrasive consumption)
- Noise generation
- Material thickness limitations for precision work

**Key References:**
- Kovacevic, R., Hashish, M., et al. (1997). "State of the art of research and development in abrasive waterjet machining." *ASME Manufacturing Science and Technology*.
- Harada, M., et al. (1993). "Reactor dismantling by abrasive water jet cutting system." *Journal Series B*.
- Amaral, M.C., et al. (2024). "Surface quality of 316L sheets after abrasive waterjet, photochemical machining, and electropolishing." *Int. J. Advanced Manufacturing Technology*.

### 1.3 ELECTROCHEMICAL ENERGY PROCESSES

#### 1.3.1 Electrochemical Machining (ECM)

**Principle:** Material is removed through controlled anodic dissolution. The workpiece (anode) and tool (cathode) are placed in an electrolyte solution. When current flows, metal atoms at the workpiece surface are oxidized and carried away as metal hydroxides by the pressurized electrolyte flow. The tool advances toward the workpiece maintaining a gap of 80-800 micrometers.

**Typical Materials Processed:**
- All electrically conductive metals
- Difficult-to-machine alloys: titanium aluminides, Inconel, Waspaloy
- High-nickel, cobalt, and rhenium alloys
- Hardened steels

**Precision/Tolerances:**
- Gap control: 80-800 micrometers
- Surface finish: mirror-quality achievable
- Complex 3D geometries reproducible
- Current density: 0.1-5 A/mm2

**Advantages:**
- Zero tool wear (single tool for unlimited parts)
- No thermal or mechanical stress on workpiece
- Excellent surface finish
- Complex concave geometries easily produced
- No burrs or residual stresses
- High material removal rates possible at high current densities

**Limitations:**
- Only conductive materials
- Corrosive electrolytes damage equipment
- High energy consumption
- Dimensional accuracy challenges (overcut prediction)
- Environmental concerns (electrolyte disposal)
- High initial equipment cost

**Key References:**
- Klocke, F., Zeis, M., Klink, A. (2015). "Interdisciplinary modelling of the electrochemical machining process for engine blades." *CIRP Annals*. (130 citations)
- Chai, M., et al. (2019). "Experimental investigations on aircraft blade cooling holes and CFD fluid analysis in ECM." *Advances in Materials Science and Engineering*.
- Jain, N.K., Jain, V.K. (2007). "Optimization of ECM process parameters using genetic algorithms." *Machining Science and Technology*.

#### 1.3.2 Electrochemical Grinding (ECG)

**Principle:** Combines electrochemical dissolution (90% of material removal) with mechanical abrasive action (10%) using a conductive grinding wheel as cathode. The workpiece is the anode, and electrolyte flows between them. The abrasive particles maintain the gap and remove the oxide/hydroxide film, enabling continuous dissolution.

**Typical Materials Processed:**
- Tungsten carbide
- Hardened tool steels
- Stainless steels
- Exotic alloys (Hastelloy, Inconel)

**Precision/Tolerances:**
- Surface finish: comparable to conventional grinding
- No thermal damage
- Minimal wheel wear

**Advantages:**
- Minimal wheel wear (10x longer than conventional)
- No thermal damage or burrs
- Good for carbides and hard alloys
- Stress-free surfaces

**Limitations:**
- Conductive materials only
- Lower MRR than conventional grinding for soft materials
- Electrolyte management required
- Higher equipment cost

### 1.4 THERMAL/ELECTRICAL ENERGY PROCESSES

#### 1.4.1 Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM / Wire-EDM)

**Principle:** Material removal occurs through high-frequency electrical discharges (sparks) between an electrode (tool) and the workpiece, both submerged in dielectric fluid. Each spark generates temperatures of 8,000-12,000 degrees C, vaporizing and melting a tiny crater of material. Hundreds of thousands of sparks per second remove material progressively.

**Types:**
- **Sinker/Ram EDM:** Shaped electrode creates 3D cavity (mirror image)
- **Wire EDM:** Thin brass wire (typically 0.25 mm diameter) cuts 2D profiles through plates up to 300 mm thick
- **Micro-EDM:** Nanoscale crater dimensions for micro-features
- **Hole-drilling EDM:** Rotating tube electrode for small deep holes

**Typical Materials Processed:**
- Any electrically conductive material regardless of hardness
- Hardened steel, tungsten carbide
- Titanium alloys, Inconel, Hastelloy
- Polycrystalline diamond (PCD)
- Conductive ceramics

**Precision/Tolerances:**
- Wire EDM: +/-1 micrometer achievable
- Minimum kerf width: 0.021 mm (wire EDM)
- Surface roughness: controlled by spark parameters (finishing passes)
- Guide accuracy: 0.004 mm

**Advantages:**
- Machines any conductive material regardless of hardness
- Complex 3D geometries (sinker) or intricate 2D profiles (wire)
- No mechanical force or distortion
- Excellent surface finish achievable
- Pre-hardened materials processed directly
- Very fine features (micro-holes, thin walls)

**Limitations:**
- Slow material removal rate
- High specific energy consumption
- Only conductive materials (generally)
- Recast layer formation at surfaces
- Tool/electrode wear (sinker EDM)
- Fire hazard with oil-based dielectrics
- Requires skilled operators

**Key References:**
- Jahan, M.P., Rahman, M., Wong, Y.S. (2011). "A review on the conventional and micro-electrodischarge machining of tungsten carbide." *Int. J. Machine Tools and Manufacture*. (342 citations)
- Tong, H., et al. (2013). "Mechanism design and process control of micro EDM for drilling spray holes of diesel injector nozzles." *Precision Engineering*.
- Li, L., et al. (2006). "Sequential laser and EDM micro-drilling for next generation fuel injection nozzle manufacture." *CIRP Annals*.

#### 1.4.2 Laser Beam Machining (LBM)

**Principle:** A high-power coherent light beam is focused to an extremely small spot (as small as 0.0125 inches / 0.32 mm), creating energy densities sufficient to melt, vaporize, or photochemically decompose material. An assist gas (oxygen, nitrogen, or air) blows away molten/vaporized material.

**Types:**
- **CO2 Lasers:** Versatile for metals and non-metals
- **Fiber Lasers:** Superior for reflective metals; spot size 100x smaller than CO2
- **Nd:YAG Lasers:** High power for boring, drilling, engraving
- **Excimer Lasers:** UV wavelength for precision micro-machining
- **Femtosecond Lasers:** Ultra-short pulses for minimal thermal damage

**Typical Materials Processed:**
- All metals (steel, aluminum, titanium, copper, brass)
- Non-metals (wood, plastics, textiles, ceramics)
- Composites (CFRP, GFRP)
- Semiconductors (silicon wafer dicing)

**Precision/Tolerances:**
- Positioning accuracy: +/-10 micrometers
- Repeatability: +/-5 micrometers
- Surface roughness (Rz): 3-6 micrometers
- Achievable tolerances: +/-0.025 mm (0.001 in)
- Kerf width: as narrow as 0.1 mm

**Cutting Speeds (CO2 laser, 1mm thickness):**
- Stainless steel: 23.28 cm/s
- Aluminum: 14.82 cm/s
- Mild steel: 8.89 cm/s

**Advantages:**
- Extremely high precision and repeatability
- Minimal heat-affected zone (especially ultrafast lasers)
- No tool wear
- Non-contact process
- Highly automatable (CNC)
- Versatile (cutting, drilling, welding, marking, hardening, texturing)
- Up to 30x faster than sawing

**Limitations:**
- High capital and operating costs
- Limited thickness capacity compared to plasma
- Power efficiency varies (CO2: 5-10%; fiber: 20-30%)
- Reflective materials challenging for some laser types
- Safety concerns (eye damage, fumes)
- High power consumption

**Key References:**
- Dubey, A.K., Yadava, V. (2008). "Laser beam machining—a review." *Int. J. Machine Tools and Manufacture*. (1,221 citations)
- Gower, M.C. (2000). "Industrial applications of laser micromachining." *Optics Express*. (357 citations)
- Powell, J., Petring, D. (2021). "High-power laser cutting." *Handbook of Laser Technology and Applications*.

#### 1.4.3 Electron Beam Machining (EBM)

**Principle:** High-velocity electrons (accelerated to ~200,000 km/s by 150-200 kV) are focused into a narrow beam by electromagnetic lenses. Upon impact, kinetic energy converts to thermal energy, vaporizing material. Operates in pulsed mode (50 microseconds to 15 milliseconds per pulse).

**Typical Materials Processed:**
- High melting point metals and alloys
- High thermal conductivity materials
- Specialty aerospace alloys

**Precision/Tolerances:**
- Superior surface finish compared to other thermal processes
- Narrower kerf width than laser or plasma
- Micro-scale features achievable

**Beam Parameters:**
- Voltage: 150-200 kV
- Beam current: 200 microamps to 1 amp
- Energy per pulse: can exceed 100 joules

**Advantages:**
- Extreme precision and fine features
- Very narrow kerf
- Excellent surface finish
- Suitable for highest-melting-point materials

**Limitations:**
- Requires high vacuum environment
- Limited to small parts (vacuum chamber size)
- Produces hazardous X-rays
- Extremely high equipment cost
- Requires highly trained operators
- Low productivity

**Key References:**
- Bergs, T., Klocke, F. (2025). "Material machining with electron beams." Springer.
- Weglowski, M.S., et al. (2016). "Electron beam welding technologies." *Vacuum*. (525 citations)

#### 1.4.4 Plasma Arc Machining/Cutting (PAM)

**Principle:** An electrical arc ionizes a compressed gas flowing through a focused nozzle, creating a plasma jet at temperatures of 20,000-30,000 degrees C. This superheated plasma melts through conductive material while high-velocity gas blows away molten metal.

**Typical Materials Processed:**
- Steel and stainless steel
- Aluminum
- Brass and copper
- Any electrically conductive metal

**Thickness Range:**
- Hand-held: up to 38 mm (1.5 in) steel
- CNC-controlled: up to 150 mm (6 in) steel

**Precision/Tolerances:**
- Cut quality inferior to laser (wider kerf, asymmetric edges)
- Kerf wider at top than bottom
- Good for thick materials where laser is impractical

**Advantages:**
- Very high cutting speed
- Low cost per cut
- Handles thick materials
- No metal chips produced
- Cleaner edges than oxy-fuel
- Good for curved/angled cuts

**Limitations:**
- Electrode wear (consumable)
- Asymmetric kerf geometry
- Inferior to laser for thin materials and holes
- Heat-affected zone present
- Limited to conductive materials
- UV radiation and fume hazards

**Key References:**
- Nemchinsky, V.A., Severance, W.S. (2006). "What we know and what we do not know about plasma arc cutting." *Journal of Physics D*. 
- Cinar, Z., et al. (2018). "Developments in plasma arc cutting of steel alloys: a review." *Jurnal Kejuruteraan*.

### 1.5 CHEMICAL ENERGY PROCESSES

#### 1.5.1 Chemical Machining (CHM) / Chemical Milling

**Principle:** Controlled removal of material through chemical dissolution by etchant solutions. Protective maskants cover areas to remain intact while exposed regions dissolve in temperature-regulated chemical baths. Material removal depth controlled by immersion time (E = s/t relationship).

**Process Steps:**
1. Cleaning (surface preparation)
2. Masking (neoprene or isobutylene-isoprene polymer coatings)
3. Scribing (CNC or manual maskant removal)
4. Etching (controlled chemical bath)
5. Demasking (cleaning and mask removal)

**Etchants by Material:**
- Aluminum: NaOH, phosphoric/nitric acid, FeCl3
- Steels: HCl/HNO3 mixtures, FeCl3, nital
- Copper: CuCl2, FeCl3, ammonium persulfate
- Silicon dioxide: HF (hydrofluoric acid)

**Typical Etch Rates:**
- Aluminum: ~0.178 cm/h
- Magnesium: ~0.46 cm/h

**Advantages:**
- No burrs or mechanical stresses
- Uniform material removal over large areas
- Suitable for thin and delicate parts
- No heat-affected zone
- Large-area simultaneous processing
- Good for weight reduction of aerospace panels

**Limitations:**
- Slow process
- Hazardous chemicals
- Maskant application is labor-intensive
- Etch rates vary and require experimental calibration
- Undercutting at mask edges
- Environmental disposal concerns

---

#### 1.5.2 Photochemical Machining (PCM) / Photo-Etching

**Principle:** Combines photolithography with chemical etching. A UV-sensitive photoresist is applied to the workpiece, exposed to UV light through a photomask defining the desired pattern, then developed to reveal areas for etching. Chemical dissolution removes exposed metal.

**Typical Materials Processed:**
- Stainless steel (including 316L for chemical equipment)
- Copper and copper alloys
- Aluminum
- Nickel alloys
- Titanium

**Precision/Tolerances:**
- Feature resolution: dependent on material thickness (typically equal to thickness)
- Very fine details reproducible
- No burrs or distortion
- Suitable for thin foils (0.01-1.5 mm)

**Advantages:**
- Extremely fine detail
- No mechanical stress or thermal damage
- Ideal for thin metal parts
- Rapid prototyping capability
- Tooling (photomasks) inexpensive vs. stamping dies
- Burr-free edges

**Limitations:**
- Limited to thin materials
- Undercutting limits minimum feature size
- Chemical waste disposal
- Not economical for thick materials
- Resolution decreases with material thickness

**Key References:**
- Allen, D.M. (2004). "Photochemical Machining: From 'Manufacturing's Best Kept Secret' to a $6 Billion Per Annum Rapid Manufacturing Process." *CIRP Annals*. (129 citations)
- Friedman, H. (1989). "Photochemical Machining." *Machining handbook*.
- Agrawal, D., Kamble, D. (2019). "Optimization of photochemical machining process parameters for manufacturing microfluidic channels." *Materials and Manufacturing Processes*. (35 citations)

## 2. DIRECT APPLICATIONS IN THE PAINT/PIGMENT PRODUCTION CHAIN

### 2.1 Laser Ablation for Nanoparticle/Nanopigment Synthesis (PLAL)

**This is the most well-documented direct application of a non-conventional machining principle within the paint/pigment industry.**

Pulsed Laser Ablation in Liquid (PLAL) is a "top-down" nanoparticle synthesis technique where a solid target is irradiated by focused pulsed laser beams while submerged in liquid, producing colloidal nanoparticles. This technique has been directly applied to synthesize pigment-grade nanoparticles:

#### 2.1.1 TiO2 Nanoparticles (White Pigment)

TiO2 is the world's most important white pigment, used in virtually all paints. PLAL synthesis of TiO2 nanoparticles has been extensively documented:

- **Fachin, L.B., de Bortoli, L.S., Santos, E., Hotza, D. (2023).** "Green synthesis of blue titania nanoparticles by pulsed laser ablation in water." *Open Ceramics*. — Produced TiO2 nanoparticles for pigment and nanocatalyst applications using an environmentally friendly approach.

- **Zuniga-Ibarra, V.A., et al. (2019).** "Synthesis and characterization of black TiO2 nanoparticles by pulsed laser irradiation in liquid." *Applied Surface Science*. (124 citations) — Used Nd:YAG laser (532 nm, 10 ns, 10 Hz) to modify white TiO2 powder in water, creating modified nanoparticles.

- **Hussain, S.A., Al-Mayalee, K.H. (2024).** "Characterization and comparison of titanium dioxide nanoparticles by microwave and pulse laser ablation liquid methods." *Journal of Kufa-Physics*. — Directly describes TiO2 as a "white pigment, nanocatalyst, or semiconductor."

- **Siuzdak, K., et al. (2014).** "Preparation of platinum modified titanium dioxide nanoparticles with laser ablation in water." *Physical Chemistry Chemical Physics*. (106 citations) — Produced "white colloid of titania nanoparticles."

- **Liang, C., et al. (2004).** "Synthesis, characterization, and phase stability of ultrafine TiO2 nanoparticles by pulsed laser ablation in liquid media." *Journal of Materials Research*. (73 citations)

#### 2.1.2 ZnO Nanoparticles (White Pigment / UV Absorber)

ZnO serves as both a white pigment and functional additive (UV protection, antibacterial) in paint formulations:

- **Foudi, H., et al. (2023).** "Synthesis and characterization of ZnO nanoparticles for antibacterial paints." *Chemical Papers*. — Directly links laser-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles to paint applications.

- **Rohani, R., et al. (2022).** "Green approaches, potentials, and applications of zinc oxide nanoparticles in surface coatings and films." *Journal of Nanomaterials and Applications*. — Notes that "physical and chemical methods such as laser ablation" enable ZnO production for coatings.

- **Zamiri, R., et al. (2012).** "Aqueous starch as a stabilizer in zinc oxide nanoparticle synthesis via laser ablation." *Journal of Alloys and Compounds*.

#### 2.1.3 Mixed Metal Oxide / Functional Pigment Nanoparticles

- **Salih, A.A., Haider, A.J., Nazari, A. (2021).** "Preparation and characterizations of nanomaterial by pulsed laser ablation in liquid (PLAIL) as friendly environment paint." *Journal of Physics: Conference Series*. — **Most directly relevant paper**: Synthesized silver/nickel oxide nanoparticles through Nd:YAG laser ablation and incorporated them directly into alkyd resin paint formulations.

- **Lau, M., Waag, F., Barcikowski, S. (2017).** "Direct integration of laser-generated nanoparticles into transparent nail polish: the plasmonic 'Goldfinger'." *Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research*. — Demonstrated direct synthesis of gold nanoparticles within a nail polish (coating) matrix.

- **Sayed, M.A., et al. (2023).** "Low-cost fabrication and physicochemical characterization of ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles as an efficient multifunctional inorganic pigment." *Journal of Coatings Technology and Research*. — Iron-oxide ferrite pigments for printing inks and protective coatings.

#### 2.1.4 PLAL Advantages for Pigment Production

According to review literature (Zhang et al., 2017, 311 citations; Yogesh et al., 2021, 144 citations; Nyabadza et al., 2023, 88 citations):

- **Surfactant-free synthesis:** No chemical contaminants in final product
- **Green chemistry:** Water as the medium, no toxic precursors
- **Controlled particle size:** Tunable by laser parameters (wavelength, pulse duration, fluence)
- **High purity:** No byproducts from chemical reactions
- **Versatility:** Any solid target can be ablated
- **Direct integration:** Nanoparticles can be generated directly in the paint/coating matrix

**Current Status:** PLAL is primarily a laboratory/research technique for nanoparticle pigments. Industrial-scale PLAL for pigment production is not yet competitive with conventional precipitation/calcination methods due to lower throughput, but it is advancing rapidly as a route to specialty nanopigments.

### 2.2 Plasma Treatment of Surfaces Before Painting

**This is the second most extensively documented application, with strong industrial adoption.**

Atmospheric and low-pressure plasma treatments modify surface chemistry and morphology to dramatically improve paint adhesion. While technically a "surface treatment" rather than "machining," the process involves controlled material removal and surface restructuring using plasma energy.

#### 2.2.1 Polypropylene (Automotive)

- **Guarnaccio, A., et al. (2021).** "Femtosecond laser surface texturing of polypropylene copolymer for automotive paint applications." *Surface and Coatings Technology*. (27 citations) — Femtosecond laser treatment significantly improved paint adhesion to polypropylene in automotive applications.

- **Peng, X., Zhang, Z. (2020).** "Polypropylene surface painting via plasma treatment." *Journal of Adhesion Science and Technology*. — Plasma treatment followed by waterborne primer and topcoat demonstrated excellent film adhesion.

#### 2.2.2 Aluminum Alloys

- **Patel, V.K., Bhowmik, S. (2017).** "Aluminum alloy plasma processing." *Reviews of Adhesion and Adhesives*. — Reviewed optimal plasma conditions for enhanced paint adhesion per MIL-P-23377 military standards.

- **Mui, T.S.M., et al. (2017).** "Aluminum alloy atmospheric pressure treatment." *Surface and Coatings Technology*. — Plasma effectively cleaned aluminum and improved polyurethane coating adhesion.

#### 2.2.3 Polymer Composites (Aerospace)

- **Atlioglu, M., Kaynak, C. (2024).** "Composite laminates paint adhesion." *Journal of Thermoplastic Composite Materials*. — Ar-O2 plasma treatment demonstrated superior primer and topcoat adherence on PPS/CF composites.

#### 2.2.4 Industrial Implementation (Plasmatreat)

- **Plasmatreat GmbH (2020).** *IST International Surface Technology*. — Documents industrial-scale plasma pretreatment replacing traditional chemical phosphating in automotive painting lines, providing cost reduction and environmental benefits.

#### 2.2.5 Mechanism

Plasma treatment works by:
1. **Surface cleaning:** Removes organic contaminants
2. **Surface activation:** Creates polar functional groups (C-O, C=O, O-H) that increase surface energy
3. **Micro-roughening:** Creates nanoscale topography for mechanical anchoring
4. **Wettability enhancement:** Increases contact angle compatibility with paint

**Key metric:** Surface energy increases from ~30 mN/m (untreated polypropylene) to >50 mN/m (plasma-treated), enabling paint wetting.

### 2.3 Laser Paint Removal/Stripping

**This is a well-established industrial application with thousands of documented implementations.**

#### 2.3.1 Aerospace Applications

Laser paint stripping is extensively used in aircraft maintenance:

- **Zhu, S., et al. (2025).** "Precision paint stripping of multi-layer aerospace coatings on CFRP using femtosecond lasers." *Composites Part B*. — Achieved ablation thresholds below 0.25 J/cm2 without substrate damage.

- **Li, W., et al. (2023).** "Removal mechanisms and microstructure characteristics of laser paint stripping on aircraft skin surface." *Photonics*. — Identified three removal mechanisms: thermal decomposition, evaporation, and spallation.

- **Zhu, G., et al. (2020).** "Corrosion and wear performance of aircraft skin after laser cleaning." *Optics & Laser Technology*. — Boeing aircraft skins cleaned with laser maintain structural integrity.

- **Li, X., et al. (2021).** "Laser paint stripping strategy in engineering application: a systematic review." *Optik*. — Comprehensive assessment of laser stripping as "advanced paint removal and repair."

#### 2.3.2 Technical Parameters

Based on the literature:
- **Wavelengths used:** 1064 nm (Nd:YAG), 532 nm (frequency-doubled)
- **Laser types:** Fiber pulsed lasers, femtosecond lasers
- **Mechanisms:** Thermal decomposition, photochemical breaking of polymer bonds, spallation from rapid thermal expansion
- **Key parameters:** Scanning speed, power density, beam overlap rate, repetition rate
- **Threshold fluence:** Below 0.25 J/cm2 for CFRP substrates (femtosecond)

#### 2.3.3 Comparison with Conventional Paint Removal

| Aspect | Chemical Stripping | Laser Stripping |
|--------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Environmental | Hazardous solvents (MEK, DCM) | No chemicals, minimal waste |
| Substrate damage | Chemical attack possible | No damage (with proper parameters) |
| Selectivity | Layer-by-layer difficult | Layer-by-layer achievable |
| Speed | Hours (soak time) | Minutes |
| Automation | Difficult | Highly automatable |
| Cost per area | Low consumable cost | High capital, low operating |

#### 2.3.4 Industrial Context for Paint Industry

Laser paint removal is relevant to the paint industry in two ways:
1. **End-user application:** Maintenance repainting requires removal of old paint (aerospace, marine, automotive)
2. **Testing/quality control:** Paint manufacturers test adhesion and durability by controlled removal

### 2.4 Plasma Treatment of Pigment Surfaces

Plasma treatment can modify pigment particle surfaces to improve dispersibility and compatibility with paint binders:

- **Pykönen, M. (2010).** "Influence of plasma modification on surface properties." — Demonstrated plasma coating effects on pigment-containing paper coatings, modifying wettability of UV varnish.

- **Pransilp, P., et al. (2016).** "Surface modification of cotton fabrics by gas plasmas." *Applied Surface Science*. — O2 plasma improved color strength for pigmented inkjet inks compared to N2 plasma.

- **Nie, H., et al. (2025).** "Progress on pigment dispersion issues." *Pigment & Resin Technology*. — Reviews surface modification mechanisms critical to dispersion including plasma-based approaches.

**Application rationale:** Hydrophobic pigments (e.g., carbon black, organic pigments) resist wetting by water-based binders. Plasma treatment introduces polar groups on pigment surfaces, improving:
- Wetting by aqueous binders
- Dispersion stability
- Color development
- Reduced grinding time in bead mills

**Current Status:** Mostly research-stage; some specialty pigment manufacturers are investigating atmospheric plasma treatment as a continuous process for pigment surface modification.

**Pattern types investigated:**
- Dimples (individual craters)
- Grooves (linear channels)
- Grids (cross-hatched patterns)

**Adhesion improvement:** Typically 2-5x improvement in pull-off adhesion strength compared to untreated surfaces.

## 3. APPLICATIONS IN EQUIPMENT MANUFACTURE FOR THE PAINT INDUSTRY

### 3.1 EDM for Complex Geometries in Bead Mill Components

Bead mills are the primary dispersion equipment in modern paint manufacturing. Key components require non-conventional machining:

#### 3.1.1 Separator Screens/Sieves

Bead mill separator screens (which retain grinding media while allowing product to pass) feature:
- Slot widths of 0.1-0.3 mm
- Complex radial or cylindrical geometries
- Materials: hardened stainless steel, tungsten carbide

**Wire EDM application:** Wire EDM is the preferred method for cutting precise narrow slots in hardened separator discs. The 0.021 mm minimum kerf width of wire EDM enables slots narrower than any conventional cutting method.

**Photochemical machining application:** For thin separator screens (foils), PCM produces precision slot patterns without mechanical stress. Allen (2004, 129 citations) documented PCM for manufacturing "corrosion-resistant filter screens from type 316 stainless steel foil (0.1 mm thick) with precision hole spacing."

#### 3.1.2 Grinding Chamber Linings

- Wire EDM cuts complex internal profiles in hardened steel or carbide linings
- EDM produces intricate internal geometries impossible to machine conventionally in pre-hardened materials

#### 3.1.3 Evidence Base

While no paper explicitly states "EDM for bead mill separators," the technical requirements (precision slots in hardened materials, complex geometries, thin features) align perfectly with wire EDM and PCM capabilities documented in the literature. Industry practice confirms this through analogous applications in fuel injector nozzles (Tong et al., 2013) and filter/screen manufacturing (Allen, 2004).

---

### 3.2 Wire EDM for Extrusion Dies (Powder Coating Production)

Twin-screw extruders are critical equipment in powder coating manufacture. Their components require precision machining:

- **Panjan, P., et al. (2001).** "PVD CrN coating for protection of extrusion dies." *Vacuum*. (90 citations) — "Dies were formed by the wire EDM technique" before applying protective coatings.

- **Ozsoy, K. (2021).** "Examining mechanical properties of profiles manufactured aluminium extrusion dies." *Measurement*. (15 citations) — Wire erosion device used in extrusion die manufacturing.

**Applications in paint industry:**
- Flat-sheet extrusion dies for powder coating production
- Twin-screw extruder barrel profiles
- Screw element profiles
- Die plates for pelletization

**Technical justification:** Extrusion dies require:
- Complex internal flow channels
- Hardened tool steel (pre-hardened to 58-62 HRC)
- Tolerances of +/-5 to +/-10 micrometers
- Smooth internal surfaces for flow optimization

Wire EDM is one of the only processes capable of cutting these profiles in pre-hardened die steel.

---

### 3.3 Laser Cutting for Disperser Blades and Impeller Components

High-speed Cowles disperser blades operate at speeds exceeding 8,000 rpm in highly abrasive pigment slurries. Manufacturing requirements include:
- Precise blade geometry (serrated edges, specific angles)
- Stainless steel or hardened alloy construction
- Minimal heat distortion during cutting
- High edge quality

**Laser cutting application:**
- CO2 and fiber laser cutting of stainless steel blade blanks (1-6 mm thickness)
- Cutting speeds for 1mm stainless: 23.28 cm/s
- Positioning accuracy: +/-10 micrometers
- No tool wear (critical for batch production)
- Heat-affected zone minimal with nitrogen assist gas

**Literature support:** Powell & Petring (2021) document extensive laser cutting of stainless steel components. Industry catalogs from disperser manufacturers (e.g., VMA-Getzmann, Netzsch) show blade geometries consistent with laser cutting capabilities.

---

### 3.4 Laser Cladding for Wear-Resistant Surfaces on Process Equipment

Laser cladding applies wear-resistant coatings to surfaces of paint manufacturing equipment subjected to abrasive pigment slurries:

#### 3.4.1 Twin-Screw Extruder Barrels

- **Leunda, J., Sanz, C., Soriano, C. (2016).** "Laser cladding strategies for producing WC reinforced NiCr coatings inside twin barrels." *Surface and Coatings Technology*. (46 citations) — Directly addresses "wear and corrosion mechanisms that arise in different sections of the barrel" using laser cladding of tungsten carbide-nickel chromium coatings.

- **Grigoroudis, K. (1996).** Cranfield University thesis — Describes "deposition of a relatively thick wear/corrosion resistant coating" for extruder barrels.

#### 3.4.2 Mixing/Agitator Equipment

- **Geiger, T. (2025).** Technology implementation for "mixer, agitator and reactor shafts" in corrosive/abrasive environments.

- **Malikongwa, K. (2019).** "Manufacturing of wear resistant clads for slurry/tailings pump impellers via laser cladding." — Laser cladding of 304L stainless steel impellers handling abrasive slurries (directly analogous to pigment dispersing equipment).

- **Kasirlou, M., et al. (2022).** "Enhancement of impeller shaft fabrication using a novel plasma technique." — Plasma-based surface treatment for abrasion resistance improvement.

#### 3.4.3 Coating Materials for Equipment

Common laser-clad materials for paint industry equipment:
- WC-NiCr (tungsten carbide in nickel-chromium matrix): extreme abrasion resistance
- Stellite alloys: corrosion + wear resistance
- NiCrBSi: general-purpose wear protection
- FeCoNiCrMo-TiC composites: high-entropy alloy coatings

### 3.5 EDM for Spray Nozzle Orifices

Paint application relies on precision spray nozzles (HVLP guns, airless spray tips, electrostatic atomizers). These nozzles contain micro-orifices that control droplet size and spray pattern:

- **Tong, H., et al. (2013).** "Mechanism design and process control of micro EDM for drilling spray holes." *Precision Engineering*. — Micro-EDM equipment for drilling spray orifices in metal alloys.

- **Rashed, C.A.A., et al. (2013).** "Water jet guided laser as an alternative to EDM for micro-drilling of fuel injection nozzles." *Journal of Manufacturing Processes*. — Compares micro-EDM to laser drilling for precision atomization nozzles.

- **Li, L., et al. (2006).** "Sequential laser and EDM micro-drilling for next generation fuel injection nozzle manufacture." *CIRP Annals*. — Achieved 42% cost reduction combining laser pre-drilling with EDM finishing.

- **Luo, H., et al. (2021).** "Comparisons in spray and atomization characteristics with/without hydro-erosive grinding in nozzle orifice." *Fuel*. — Studies EDM-machined nozzle orifices and their atomization performance.

**Orifice specifications for paint spray equipment:**
- Airless spray tips: 0.1-0.8 mm orifice diameter
- HVLP nozzles: 0.5-2.0 mm fluid passages
- Electrostatic rotary atomizers: precision channels

**Manufacturing approach:** Micro-EDM produces the initial orifice geometry in hardened tungsten carbide or tool steel nozzle tips. The process achieves:
- Hole diameters: 50-800 micrometers
- Aspect ratios: up to 20:1
- No burrs (critical for flow uniformity)
- Consistent geometry (critical for spray pattern reproducibility)

### 3.8 Laser Hardening of Wear Surfaces

Laser transformation hardening creates a martensitic surface layer (hardness increase of 50-100%) on iron-based alloys without affecting bulk properties:

- **Gnanamuthu, D.S. (1980).** "Laser surface treatment." *Optical Engineering*. (175 citations) — "Laser transformation hardening of iron base alloys generates a hard martensitic surface which provides increased wear resistance."

- **Pantelis, D.I., et al. (2002).** "Wear and corrosion resistance of laser surface hardened structural steel." *Surface and Coatings Technology*. (181 citations)

- **Lach, L. (2024).** "Recent advances in laser surface hardening." *Crystals*. (48 citations)

**Applications in paint industry equipment:**
- Disperser shaft bearing surfaces
- Bead mill rotor surfaces
- Pump impeller edges
- Agitator blade leading edges
- Extruder screw flight edges

**Advantages over conventional hardening:**
- Selective (only wear zones treated)
- Minimal distortion (localized heating)
- No quenchant required
- CNC/robotic control for complex geometries
- Deeper hardened zones than induction hardening

**Components manufactured by EDM in WC:**

## 7. CONCLUSIONS AND SEMINAR STRUCTURE RECOMMENDATIONS

### 7.1 Key Thesis Statement

Non-conventional machining methods intersect with the paint and pigment industry at **three distinct levels**: (1) in the synthesis of advanced nanopigments via laser ablation; (2) in surface preparation, paint application, and quality control operations using plasma, laser, and ultrasonic technologies; and (3) in the manufacture of critical equipment components (bead mill separators, extrusion dies, spray nozzles, disperser blades, reactor vessels) where EDM, wire EDM, laser cutting, laser cladding, plasma cutting, and photochemical machining enable geometries and material processing impossible with conventional methods.

### 7.2 Parallel to Conventional Machining Seminar

The conventional machining seminar established that turning, milling, drilling, grinding, and threading appear in paint-industry EQUIPMENT manufacturing. The non-conventional machining story adds:

1. **A direct production-chain application** that conventional machining lacks: laser ablation for nanopigment synthesis
2. **Critical enabling technologies** for paint/coating application quality: plasma/laser surface preparation
3. **Equipment manufacturing applications** that complement and extend conventional machining, particularly for hardened materials, micro-features, and complex geometries

### 7.3 Suggested Seminar Structure (12 pages)

1. Introduction (1 page): Context, definitions, why non-conventional methods exist
2. Classification of Non-Conventional Methods (2 pages): Table + brief descriptions by energy type
3. Direct Applications in Paint/Pigment Production (3 pages): PLAL nanopigments, plasma treatment of pigments, laser paint removal
4. Applications in Surface Preparation and Quality (2 pages): Plasma/laser for adhesion, ultrasonic testing
5. Equipment Manufacturing Applications (3 pages): EDM/Wire-EDM, laser cutting, laser cladding, PCM, plasma cutting
6. Conclusion (1 page): Synthesis and future trends

### 7.4 Honest Assessment of Evidence Gaps

- No paper explicitly titled "Non-conventional machining in the paint industry" exists
- The connection between EDM and bead mill separators is inferred from technical capability matching, not from explicit industry documentation
- PLAL for pigments remains primarily laboratory-scale
- Most equipment manufacturing connections require reasoning from analogous applications (fuel injectors -> paint spray nozzles; turbine blades -> disperser blades)
- The strongest, most directly documented connections are: laser paint removal, plasma surface pretreatment, ultrasonic coating testing, and laser drilling of inkjet nozzles
